# Seniors housing playbook

Engine: operating. Residents pay monthly for housing and, in most subtypes, services and care. The operator and the license matter as much as the building.

## 1. What it is

| Subtype | Notes |
|---------|-------|
| Independent living | Apartments plus dining and activities. Little or no care. Often not licensed as care. Closest to multifamily |
| Assisted living | Help with daily activities. State licensed. Care fees on top of rent |
| Memory care | Secured units for dementia. Higher staffing, higher rates, state licensed |
| Skilled nursing | Medical care, heavy regulation, government payer mix. Treat as a separate specialty |
| Continuing care community | Multiple levels on one campus, often with entrance fees |
| Active adult | Age-restricted housing without services. Treat mostly as multifamily |

## 2. How income is produced

- Residents sign a residency agreement, usually month to month with a 30 day notice. There is no lease term to rely on.
- Income is base rent, a community fee, and care fees by level of care. Many communities charge for meals and extras.
- Most residents pay privately. Some pay with long-term care insurance or government programs. Know the mix.
- The owner usually hires an operator under a management agreement, or leases the building to an operator.
- With a management agreement, the owner takes operating risk. With an operator lease, the owner takes credit risk on the operator and gets rent coverage tests.

## 3. Key metrics

| Metric | How to compute | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|----------------|
| Occupancy | Occupied units / total units | Volume. Track by unit type and care level |
| Revenue per occupied unit | Total resident revenue / occupied units | Pricing and care mix |
| Care level mix | Residents by care level / total residents | Care drives revenue and cost |
| Average length of stay | Months in residence, by level | Sets how many move-ins are needed |
| Move-ins and move-outs | Count per month | Leading indicator for occupancy |
| Operating margin | Net operating income before management fee / revenue | Unit economics of the community |
| Labor cost per occupied unit | Total labor cost / occupied units | Largest expense. Checks staffing |
| Agency labor share | Contract staff cost / total labor cost | Cost and care quality risk |
| EBITDAR coverage | Net income before rent and fees / rent (operator lease) | Operator's ability to pay |
| Value per unit or per bed | Price / units | Compare with replacement cost |

## 4. Revenue drivers

- Unit count, unit mix (studio, one bedroom, shared), and rate by type.
- Occupancy ramp, move-in pace, length of stay.
- Care fees: level of care assessments, how often residents move up, and how care is priced (levels or points).
- Annual rate increases and how well residents accept them. Resident-facing rate notices are regulated in many states.
- Community fees and second person fees.
- Referral sources: hospitals, physician offices, placement agents, local community, family.

## 5. Expense drivers

- Labor: caregivers, nurses, dining, housekeeping, maintenance. Typically the largest line. Minimum staffing rules and wage pressure matter.
- Food, supplies, utilities, repairs.
- Sales and marketing: the cost to fill a unit, including referral fees.
- Insurance: general and professional liability, workers compensation. Claims history drives cost.
- Management fee: percent of revenue, often with an incentive fee. Check which costs the operator passes through.
- Property tax: often reassessed on sale. Some owners claim exemptions, so check eligibility.

## 6. Capital needs

- Replacement reserve, rule of thumb, verify locally. Reserve per unit is a better test than percent of revenue.
- Unit refresh on turnover, and larger renovations to modernize older buildings.
- Life safety: sprinklers, alarms, generators, nurse call systems, secure memory care doors.
- Conversion capital to change unit mix, for example independent to assisted, or add memory care.
- Working capital and lease-up losses for new or newly repositioned buildings. Fill-up takes time and burns cash.

## 7. Debt and lender view

- Lenders: banks, life companies, agency lenders for qualified communities, specialty lenders, debt funds.
- Sizing: DSCR and debt yield on NOI after a market management fee and reserve. Lenders require licensing in good standing.
- Operator quality weighs heavily. Lenders often require an approved operator and may need consent to a change.
- Operator lease deals add rent coverage tests.
- Quirks: license assignment on foreclosure, regulatory approval of new owners and operators, operator replacement rights, reserves for lease-up, entrance fee escrow.

## 8. Screening quick tests

| Test | Pass looks like |
|------|-----------------|
| Price per unit against replacement cost | Below build cost, with a reason that new supply is not cheaper |
| Occupancy trend | Stable or rising over 24 months. Explain any gap |
| Operating margin | In line with similar communities at this care level |
| Operator | Experience in this subtype and market, clean record, a track record of lease-up |
| License status | In good standing, no unresolved findings |
| Payer mix | Mostly private pay, or government dependency is priced |

## 9. Diligence additions

- Licenses, permits and certificates of occupancy. Confirm whether licenses transfer to a new owner or a new operator, and the timeline. Flag for counsel.
- State inspection reports and citations for three to five years, and plans of correction.
- Management or lease agreement: term, fees, termination on sale, performance tests, owner approval of budgets.
- Resident roll: unit, move-in date, care level, rate, payer, notice of move-out.
- Trailing 24 months of occupancy by unit type, move-ins, move-outs and reasons.
- Staffing: headcount, turnover, vacancies, agency spend, labor agreements, ratios against regulations.
- Incident and claims history, litigation, insurance loss runs.
- Resident agreement forms, rate increase history, deposit and refund policy.
- Building condition: life safety, accessibility, older systems, fire inspections.
- Entrance fee obligations and refund schedule, if any.

## 10. Red flags

- Occupancy falling while rate rises.
- Operator with a record of citations, closures or lease defaults.
- License at risk, or ownership change that triggers a new application.
- High agency labor, high turnover, open management roles.
- Margins held up only by deferred maintenance or low staffing.
- Large care-level shift not matched by pricing.
- Operator lease with thin coverage and no guarantor.
- New supply in the primary market area.
- Review patterns about neglect, falls, missing medication or staff shortages.

## 11. Value levers

- Fill vacant units with a better sales and referral process.
- Reprice care levels so rate follows care delivered.
- Add or convert units to memory care or assisted living where demand supports it.
- Replace the operator when performance is weak, with licensing timeline built in.
- Reduce agency labor and turnover.
- Refresh units to move rate.
- Renegotiate the management or lease agreement at renewal.

## 12. Local intelligence

- Online reviews on senior living directories, maps, and employer sites: score trend, volume, owner response, patterns on staffing, care, food and cleanliness. Read patterns, never name staff or residents.
- State licensing agency: inspection reports, citations, enforcement actions, complaint history.
- Competing communities: rates, occupancy, new openings, permits, certificate of need rules where they apply.
- Age 75 and over population and household income in the primary market area, and the share of residents who can afford private pay. Census data.

## 13. Model inputs to capture

- Units and beds by type and care level, year built, license capacity.
- Occupancy by year and by care level. Move-ins and move-outs per month. Length of stay.
- Base rent by unit type, community fee, care fee by level, annual rate increase.
- Other revenue: ancillary services, second person fees.
- Departmental expense percent of revenue: labor by department, food, supplies, utilities, repairs.
- Undistributed expense percent of revenue: administrative, sales and marketing, insurance.
- Management fee percent and incentive fee. Reserve per unit. Property tax on reassessed value.
- Lease-up schedule, operating deficit funding, renovation capital and timing.
- If an operator lease: rent, escalator, coverage test, guarantor.
- Exit cap rate, sale cost, hold period.
- Debt: amount, rate, interest-only period, amortization, reserve requirements.

## 14. Site visit and management questions

- Visit at a meal time and at shift change. Watch staff and residents.
- Walk the memory care area, the kitchen, the med room, and one unit not yet refreshed.
- Ask the director how many residents moved out in the last year and why.
- Ask how care levels are assessed and when rates change.
- Ask about the last state survey and what was fixed.
- Ask how many positions are open and how much agency staff is used.
- Ask what the next competitor opening would do to move-ins.
- Ask how the operator is paid, in total, including fees from the community.
