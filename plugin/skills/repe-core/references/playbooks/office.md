# Playbook: Office

Engine: `lease` (lease-by-lease). Read with `metrics-and-formulas.md` and `standards.md`.

## 1. What it is

| Subtype | Description | Main risk |
|---------|-------------|-----------|
| CBD high-rise | Multi-tenant tower, long floorplates, many lease expirations | Demand shifts, large block vacancy |
| Suburban low-rise | Garden or mid-rise, surface parking, local and regional tenants | Obsolescence, thin tenant pool |
| Creative / conversion | Older brick-and-beam or converted space | Capex, narrow tenant demand |
| Single-tenant office | One tenant, often long term | See net-lease playbook for credit and residual |
| Office with ground floor retail | Mixed use with small retail component | Retail co-tenancy and use conflicts |

## 2. How income is produced

- Rent from a tenant table. Each lease has its own SF, rent, bumps, term and options.
- Structures: full service gross with a base year, modified gross, and NNN. Know which one each tenant has.
- Gross leases pay expense growth above a base year. Base year choice sets how much recovery you really collect.
- Parking, storage, antenna and signage income are separate lines. Confirm each has a written agreement.
- Free rent and abated rent make cash rent differ from straight-line rent. Underwrite cash.

## 3. Key metrics

| Metric | How to compute | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|----------------|
| WALT | Remaining term weighted by rent | Sets how long income is locked in |
| Rollover by year | Rent expiring each year / total rent | Shows when re-leasing cost hits |
| Mark to market | Market rent / in-place rent minus 1 | Shows upside or downside at rollover |
| Net effective rent | PV of rent less free rent, TI and commissions, per SF per year | Compares deals fairly |
| Leased vs occupied | Signed SF vs SF with tenants in place | Gap holds free rent and unpaid deals |
| Tenant concentration | Top 5 tenants as share of rent | Single exit can break the loan |
| Occupancy cost per SF | Rent plus recoveries per SF | Tests whether rent can grow |
| Parking ratio | Stalls per 1,000 SF | Limits leasing to dense users |

## 4. Revenue drivers

- Contract rent and annual bumps (fixed percent, fixed dollar steps, or CPI with caps).
- Rollover: renewal probability, downtime, free rent and market rent at expiry.
- Expense recoveries and the base year or expense stop on each lease.
- Sublease space in the building competes with your direct space.
- Expansion, contraction and early termination rights that tenants may use.

## 5. Expense drivers

- Property tax: often the largest line. Model reassessment on sale where the jurisdiction reassesses.
- Insurance, including terrorism or flood where required.
- Utilities, janitorial, security and elevator contracts.
- Repairs and maintenance, landscaping, snow removal.
- Management fee as a percent of EGI. Add on-site staff if separate.
- Reserves sit below NOI: structure, roof, HVAC and elevators.

## 6. Capital needs

- TI allowances: new tenants cost far more than renewals. Model both.
- Leasing commissions: new vs renewal, with outside broker splits.
- Base building: lobby, restrooms, elevators, HVAC, life safety and amenity space.
- Spec suites to compete with new buildings.
- Energy and code compliance upgrades required by local rules.
- Leasing capital is usually the largest cash need in the hold. Fund it at close.

## 7. Debt and lender view

- Lenders: banks, life companies, debt funds, CMBS conduits.
- Debt yield and DSCR usually bind, not LTV.
- Lenders haircut NOI for near-term rollover. Many require leasing reserves and cash sweeps before big expirations.
- Rule of thumb, verify locally: lenders size to in-place leases only, not to the lease-up plan.
- Watch recourse carve-outs, lockbox triggers and tenant-in-place tests for anchor leases.
- Floating-rate bridge debt needs extension tests that a half-vacant building may fail.

## 8. Screening quick tests

| Test | Pass looks like |
|------|-----------------|
| WALT vs hold period | Enough term, or a clear re-leasing plan funded at close |
| Largest rollover year | No single year above what the debt can carry |
| Mark to market | In-place not far above market without a bump plan |
| Basis vs replacement cost | Price per SF below cost to build new |
| Tenant concentration | No tenant can close the deal if it leaves |
| Leasing capital | Total TI and commissions fit in the budget |

## 9. Diligence additions

- Read every lease. Abstract options, termination rights, ROFR, ROFO, exclusives and expansion rights.
- Tie rent roll to leases, estoppels and bank deposits.
- Run the base year reconciliation. Check recovery audits for open tenant disputes.
- Review CAM reconciliations for the last three years.
- Get tenant financials for any tenant above 10 percent of rent.
- Check parking: counts, agreements, ratio and reserved stalls promised.
- Property condition report on building systems. Environmental report.
- Review sublease and assignment status. Who pays the head lease?

## 10. Red flags

- Large tenant with a termination option in the near term.
- Tenant in a shrinking industry, or one that has announced layoffs or consolidation.
- Rent roll with heavy free rent that makes occupancy look better than cash.
- Sublease space growing in the building.
- Deferred maintenance on elevators, HVAC or the roof.
- Rents in place above market with no bump to market planned.
- Landlord TI obligations not yet paid on recent leases.
- Property tax appeals that mask a reassessment risk.

## 11. Value levers

- Lease up vacancy with spec suites and an amenity upgrade.
- Early renewals of large tenants at fair rent with term.
- Mark below-market leases to market as they roll.
- Fix recovery leakage: base years, caps and uncaptured charges.
- Repurpose weak floors to flex or coworking-style short-term space.
- Convert to another use if zoning supports it (counsel review).

## 12. Local intelligence

- Tenant news first: credit events, layoffs, relocations, mergers, headquarters moves, return-to-office policy changes.
- Government lease actions and agency space consolidations.
- New supply: deliveries, groundbreakings and pre-leasing at competing buildings.
- Sublease listings in the submarket.
- Large block availabilities and who is touring them.
- County tax records, permits, and court filings against tenants.

## 13. Model inputs to capture

| Input | Notes |
|-------|-------|
| Tenant, suite, SF | One row per lease |
| Lease start, expiry, options | Include notice dates |
| Base rent per SF and bumps | Fixed or CPI, with cap |
| Lease structure | Gross, modified gross, NNN, base year |
| Market rent per SF | By floor and size band |
| Renewal probability | Use tenant-level, not blanket |
| Downtime months | After expiry, before new rent |
| Free rent months | New vs renewal |
| TI per SF | New vs renewal |
| Leasing commission | New vs renewal, percent of rent |
| Recoveries | Per SF, base year, caps |
| Opex lines | Tax, insurance, utilities, R&M, payroll, management |
| Tax reassessment | Year and assessed value after sale |
| Reserves per SF | Building systems |
| Termination fees | Amount and timing |

## 14. Site visit and management questions

- Walk every floor. Count dark suites, sublease signs and tenants moving out.
- Ask which tenants are shrinking, growing or in talks with other buildings.
- Ask why the last three tenants left and why the last three signed.
- Check lobby, elevators, restrooms and loading. First impressions drive tours.
- Ask about open TI obligations, disputes and CAM audits.
- Ask who the building competes with and what incentives they offer.
