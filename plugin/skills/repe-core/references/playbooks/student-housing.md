# Playbook: Student Housing

Engine: `unit`. Leases are short, often by the bed, and income depends on one university and one leasing season.

## 1. What it is

| Subtype | Description | Underwriting focus |
|---------|-------------|--------------------|
| Purpose-built off-campus | Bed-by-bed leases, individual liability, furnished | Pre-lease pace, university enrollment, distance to campus |
| Conventional near campus | Apartments with heavy student mix | Mix of students and non-students, rent per bed vs per unit |
| On-campus (public-private) | Ground lease or availability payments with a university | Contract terms, university credit, reversion |
| Dorm and co-living | Shared rooms or suites, managed by university or operator | Occupancy by term, operating intensity |
| Pre-leased development | Delivered for a fall start | Delivery date risk, entitlement, construction timeline |

## 2. How income is produced

- Rent per bed, on 9 to 12 month leases that start in a fixed window, usually late summer.
- Parental guaranty is common. Check enforceability and the share of leases with a guarantor.
- Other income: parking, furniture packages, utilities, fees, summer rent, retail.
- Unlike conventional apartments, leasing is concentrated in a few months. Missing the season costs the whole year.
- Engine inputs: bed or unit counts, SF, in-place and market rent per bed, renovation premium and pace.

## 3. Key metrics

| Metric | How to compute | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|----------------|
| Pre-lease percent | Signed beds / total beds, at fixed dates each year | Best early read on the next fall |
| Occupancy at term start | Occupied beds / total beds, at fall and spring | Season miss is hard to recover |
| Rent per bed | Total rent / beds | Compare across unit types and comps |
| Rent per SF per bed | Rent per bed / SF per bed | Normalizes different layouts |
| Enrollment share | Beds / enrolled undergraduates, within a radius | Shows supply depth |
| Beds per student housed | Purpose-built beds / students not in university housing | Supply-demand balance |
| Distance and transit time | Walking or shuttle time to the main campus | Key to pricing power |
| Summer revenue | Summer rent / annual rent | Often a sizeable swing line |
| Turn cost per bed | Make-ready cost / beds turned | Heavy wear, high turn |

## 4. Revenue drivers

- University enrollment, admission policy and housing requirements.
- Distance to campus, and shuttle or bus service.
- New purpose-built supply and the share of students living off campus.
- Pricing relative to competing beds, fall and spring.
- Leasing season execution: early bird pricing, marketing, tours.
- Greek housing, athletics and program changes that shift demand.
- Summer programs, conferences and sublease income.

## 5. Expense drivers

- Property tax: check reassessment on sale, and whether exemptions tie to university ownership.
- Insurance: higher for party and fire risk. Verify liquor, assault and habitability coverage.
- Marketing: heavy and front-loaded in the leasing season.
- Payroll: resident assistants, leasing team, maintenance. Higher turnover each summer.
- Utilities: often inclusive in rent. Model cap and overage policy.
- Repairs and turn: furniture, flooring, paint, walls. Heavy annual make-ready.
- Management fee: include corporate overhead charged to the property.

## 6. Capital needs

- Annual furniture replacement, plus flooring and fixtures. Reserves higher than conventional multifamily.
- Common area and amenity refresh, since students shop on amenities.
- Technology: internet, access control, cameras, package lockers.
- Life safety: sprinkler, alarms, egress, and balcony inspection.
- Parking structure repair or lot reconfiguration.

## 7. Debt and lender view

- Lenders: banks, debt funds, life companies, and agency lenders for qualifying assets. Many agency programs treat student housing as a special product with limits.
- Usually binding test: debt yield or DSCR on lender-adjusted NOI. LTV binds on pre-lease risk.
- Quirks: pre-lease covenants, completion guaranty, enrollment-linked reserves, shorter amortization, holdbacks until occupancy tests are met.
- On-campus deals: lender looks at the university contract and reversion terms.

## 8. Screening quick tests

- Pre-lease percent against the same date last year.
- Price per bed against replacement cost per bed.
- Distance to campus against competing beds.
- Enrollment trend and housing policy changes.
- Pipeline of competing beds opening in the next two fall seasons.
- Yield on cost against exit cap plus spread, for development.
- Share of non-student tenants and their effect on lender classification.

## 9. Diligence additions

- Lease-by-lease pre-lease report, with guarantor, rent, start date and concession.
- Prior three years of pre-lease curves by week.
- University enrollment data, housing policy and any on-campus bed plans.
- Sampling of leases for guarantor documentation and enforcement history.
- Conduct and police records near the property, noise complaints, fire incidents.
- Insurance claims, particularly fire, alcohol-related and injury claims.
- Furniture inventory and condition. Lease or ownership of the furniture.
- Zoning and occupancy limits (unrelated persons rules, bedroom counts).
- Property condition on balconies, stairs, fire systems and parking.
- Any university affiliation, naming rights or agreements, reviewed by counsel.

## 10. Red flags

- Pre-lease behind prior year at the same date with no clear reason.
- One university supplies nearly all demand and enrollment is flat or falling.
- Heavy concessions or rent cuts to hit pre-lease.
- Large supply delivering within walking distance of campus.
- University adds beds, ends a housing requirement or changes policy.
- Low guarantor coverage or poor collections.
- Review patterns showing safety, noise or maintenance trouble.
- Damage and turn costs missing from the T-12.
- Distance or transit gap to campus.
- A seller who changed the leasing calendar or pricing structure recently.

## 11. Value levers

- Fix leasing execution: earlier pricing, better tours, stronger marketing.
- Renovate units and amenities tested against rent per bed.
- Add summer programs, parking income or retail.
- Improve management and turn efficiency.
- Reposition to graduate, professional or international students where supply is thin.
- Cut utility cost with submetering or caps.
- Buy below replacement cost near an enrollment-stable campus.

## 12. Local intelligence

- Reviews: read for maintenance response, noise, roommate matching, safety, and management. Track trend and season. Do not name students or staff.
- University sources: enrollment reports, housing policy, strategic plan, dorm projects, parking and transit changes.
- New supply: city permit filings, planning agendas, and developer announcements near campus.
- Employer and campus news: program cuts, new colleges, research expansions, athletics.
- Public data: Census, university enrollment databases, county tax and code enforcement records.
- Walk the competing set in the leasing season and note concessions.

## 13. Model inputs to capture

- Unit and bed types: count, SF, beds per unit, in-place and market rent per bed.
- Renovation program: units per year, cost, premium, downtime.
- Pre-lease and occupancy by term. Vacancy, concessions, bad debt, guarantor status.
- Other income: parking, furniture, summer rent, fees, utility charges.
- Expense lines: taxes, insurance, marketing, payroll, utilities, repairs and turn, furniture, administrative, management fee.
- Tax reassessment value and exemptions.
- Reserves per bed. Capital plan by year.
- Rent growth by year, enrollment assumption, exit cap and sale costs.
- Debt terms, covenants and holdbacks.

## 14. Site visit and management questions

- Visit in the leasing season and in a quiet period. Compare activity.
- Walk the route to campus at class change time.
- How does pre-lease compare with last year by week?
- What discounts or incentives were used?
- What share of leases carry a guarantor?
- What damage and turn cost per bed do you see each year?
- What are the top resident complaints?
- How do you handle noise, parties and safety calls?
- What does the university say to you about housing policy and enrollment?
- What changes would you make to the calendar and pricing?
