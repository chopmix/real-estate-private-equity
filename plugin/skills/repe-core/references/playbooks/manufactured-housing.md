# Playbook: Manufactured Housing

Engine: `unit`. Income is mostly lot rent on land the owner controls, with homes owned by residents or by the community.

## 1. What it is

| Subtype | Description | Underwriting focus |
|---------|-------------|--------------------|
| All-age community | Mixed ages, family and workforce residents | Lot rent growth, occupancy, home quality |
| Age-restricted (55+) community | Older residents, longer tenancy | Legal compliance, resident health and turnover |
| Resident-owned (cooperative) | Residents own the land | Governance, reserves, lender view |
| Rental-home community | Owner owns homes and rents them | Home cost, repair, vacancy, financing |
| Recreational vehicle (RV) park | Short-stay and seasonal pads | Seasonality, operating business traits |
| Infill and expansion lots | Vacant pads to be filled | Home placement cost, utilities, permits |

## 2. How income is produced

- Lot rent from residents who own their homes. Moving a home is costly, so tenancy is long.
- Home rental income where the owner owns the homes. This is closer to single-family rental.
- Other income: utility reimbursement, late fees, storage, home sales and commissions, pet fees.
- Few lease expirations. Pricing follows annual notices subject to local rules.
- Engine inputs: lot or home types, counts, in-place and market lot rent, home rent, infill program and pace.

## 3. Key metrics

| Metric | How to compute | Why it matters |
|--------|----------------|----------------|
| Occupancy | Occupied lots / total lots | Primary measure of community health |
| Resident-owned home share | Resident-owned homes / occupied lots | Lot rent is stickier than home rent |
| Lot rent per month | Collected lot rent / occupied lots | Base revenue per pad |
| Rent to market | In-place lot rent / market lot rent | Pricing room or overreach |
| Expense per lot | Operating expenses / total lots | Watch utility and repair load |
| Cost per infill lot | Placement cost / lots filled | Return on filling empty pads |
| Yield on infill | Added NOI / cost per lot | Core of the value play |
| Turnover | Move-outs / occupied lots | Usually low |
| Delinquency | Past-due rent / billed rent | Sign of resident stress and enforcement |

## 4. Revenue drivers

- Annual lot rent increases, and local limits on them.
- Occupancy, driven by pad fill and abandoned homes.
- Home sales and rentals as a path to fill lots.
- Utility billing and collection, where permitted.
- Demand from affordable housing shortage nearby, and supply of competing communities.

## 5. Expense drivers

- Property tax: usually lower per pad than apartments. Check reassessment on sale.
- Insurance: wind, flood and fire exposure, and aging structures. Quotes beat trailing actuals.
- Utilities: water, sewer, and electric infrastructure. Master-metered systems expose owner to leaks and rate increases.
- Repairs and maintenance: roads, drainage, trees, and clubhouse.
- Payroll: on-site manager and maintenance.
- Management fee and administrative costs.
- Compliance costs for water and wastewater systems.

## 6. Capital needs

- Roads, drainage, lighting and utility infrastructure, often deferred.
- Water and sewer systems, wells, septic or treatment plants, and meters.
- Electric distribution, pedestals and gas lines.
- Infill: home placement, site prep, utility hookups, permits and landscaping.
- Home purchase and repair where the owner owns homes.
- Reserves per lot, scaled to infrastructure age.

## 7. Debt and lender view

- Lenders: agency lenders (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac) for qualifying communities, banks, debt funds, life companies.
- Usually binding test: DSCR on lender NOI, with a minimum occupancy and a minimum lot count. Resident-owned and age-restricted communities may have extra rules.
- Quirks: lender limits on percent of rental homes, infrastructure and environmental conditions, escrow for repairs, and cap on infill funding.

## 8. Screening quick tests

- Occupancy and trend against competing communities.
- Lot rent against comps and against alternatives for residents.
- Share of owner-owned homes. A high share of rental homes changes the risk profile.
- Utility structure: master-metered versus individually metered.
- Infrastructure age and known deferred repairs.
- Yield on infill against cost to fill.
- Reassessed tax against the T-12.
- Exposure to local rent limits and tenant protection rules.

## 9. Diligence additions

- Lot-by-lot rent roll with home ownership, tenancy dates, rent, delinquency and arrears.
- Home title and ownership records, lien search and abandoned home list.
- Phase I environmental, including septic, wells, underground tanks and fill.
- Water and sewer inspection, permits, compliance history, and capacity.
- Survey of roads, drainage, and flood risk.
- Utility billing practices and legality, reviewed by counsel.
- State and local manufactured home community law, rent notice rules and eviction rules, reviewed by counsel.
- Park rules, notices and enforcement history.
- Age-restriction compliance for 55+ communities, reviewed by counsel.
- Zoning, nonconforming use status and expansion rights.
- Home condition, roof, skirting, and tie-down on a sample of homes.
- Any pending change in ownership rules, tenant purchase rights or opportunity to purchase laws, reviewed by counsel.

## 10. Red flags

- Infrastructure deferred with no engineering report.
- Aging water or septic systems with compliance notices.
- Large share of abandoned or condemned homes.
- Heavy reliance on owner-owned rental homes with poor condition.
- Rapid lot rent increases that are above resident means or local limits.
- Master-metered utilities with no recovery.
- Zoning that does not allow expansion or replacement of homes.
- Regulatory limits on rent or resident purchase rights.
- Reviews showing unsafe conditions, dark streets or neglect.
- Seller who increased rents sharply just before sale.

## 11. Value levers

- Fill vacant lots through infill or home placement.
- Raise lot rent to market in line with legal and resident capacity.
- Convert master-metered utilities to billed or submetered, where legal.
- Replace owner-owned rental homes with resident-owned homes over time.
- Improve infrastructure, safety and curb appeal.
- Reduce expense through water loss repair and vendor consolidation.

## 12. Local intelligence

- Reviews: read for road, water, lighting, safety, and management responsiveness. Track trends. Do not name residents or staff.
- New supply: planning agendas for new communities, and zoning change notices. Supply is limited, so a single new project matters.
- Employer news: workforce demand drives residents. Track plant openings and closings.
- Public data: state environmental and utility regulator records, county records, FEMA flood maps, code enforcement.
- Local and state legislation on rent notices, rent limits and resident purchase rights.

## 13. Model inputs to capture

- Lot and home types: count, in-place lot rent, market lot rent, home rent if owned.
- Infill program: lots filled per year, cost per lot, rent after fill, ramp-up.
- Vacancy, delinquency, bad debt, abandoned home removal.
- Other income: utility billing, fees, storage, home sales.
- Expense lines: taxes, insurance, utilities, water and sewer, repairs, payroll, administrative, management fee.
- Tax reassessment value and millage.
- Reserves per lot, and infrastructure capital plan by year.
- Lot rent growth by year and cap by law if any. Exit cap rate and sale costs.
- Debt terms, repair escrows and infill funding.

## 14. Site visit and management questions

- Walk every road, and see drains and lighting at dusk if possible.
- Where do water and sewer lines run, and who maintains them?
- How many lots are vacant and why?
- How many homes are owner-owned versus resident-owned?
- Who sets lot rent and how do residents respond?
- What are the biggest complaints from residents?
- What is the process for abandoned homes and evictions?
- What permits and approvals are needed to place a home?
- What are the water or septic compliance records?
- Which capital items has the owner deferred?
