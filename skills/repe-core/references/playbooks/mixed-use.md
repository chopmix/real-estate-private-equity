# Mixed-use playbook

This file shows how to combine components. Detailed knowledge sits in each component's playbook. Do not underwrite a mixed-use building as one blended number.

## 1. What it is

| Form | Notes |
|------|-------|
| Vertical (retail below, residential or office above) | Shared structure, entrance, parking, systems |
| Horizontal (several buildings on one site) | Easier to separate and finance |
| Master-planned district | Phases, shared infrastructure, owner association |

## 2. How income is produced

- Each component has its own engine: leases for retail and office, units for apartments, operating for hotels and seniors, sell-out for condominiums.
- Components share some costs, services, parking and access. These must be assigned to a component or held as shared.

## 3. How to split into components

- List each use: square feet, units or keys, floors, revenue source, and who operates it.
- Build one schedule per component with its own rent roll or operating statement.
- Assign every revenue line and expense line to one component. Hold the rest in a shared line.
- Check that components add up to the total: SF, revenue, expense, NOI and cost.
- Read the playbook for each component type before screening that part.

## 4. Shared costs and allocation

- Shared items: structure, roof, garage, lobby, elevators, security, utilities, management, insurance, property tax, common area.
- Allocation methods: by SF, by revenue, by direct use, or by the governing agreement. State the method and who decides.
- Check the declaration, reciprocal easement agreement or association documents for required splits and approval rights.
- Test each component after its share. Retail that looks strong may be carrying costs for the residential.

## 5. Different leases and operations

- Retail and office: tenant leases, recoveries, rollover, tenant improvements and commissions. Read the lease playbooks.
- Check conflicts: noise, hours, odors, deliveries, loading and parking between uses.
- Operating agreements: who runs shared systems and who pays when the budget is exceeded.

## 6. Debt and lender view

- Different components may need different lenders and loan types, or one blended loan with a stricter test.
- Sizing: size each component on its own NOI, using its own test and rate. Sum the results, then compare with a blended loan.
- Lenders often haircut the weakest component, such as retail or hotel income.
- Quirks: release prices if components are sold separately, cross-default, cash management, reserves for tenant costs.

## 7. Screening quick tests

| Test | Pass looks like |
|------|-----------------|
| Components tie to total | SF, NOI and cost reconcile |
| Each component's own screen | No component fails its own playbook tests |
| Shared costs | Allocated by a stated method with documents behind it |
| Blended value vs sum of parts | Sum of parts is the base case, blended is the check |

## 8. Diligence additions

- Governing documents: declaration, easements, reciprocal agreements, association budget, voting rights.
- Rent roll or operating statement for each component, tied to total.
- Shared cost schedule with prior year actuals and allocation test.
- Parking study: spaces, peak demand by use, rights and leases.
- Zoning for each use, use limits, and approvals needed for any change.
- Condominium sales plan if any. Read the for-sale playbook.

## 9. Red flags

- A component that carries most of the value but is the least stable.
- Shared costs allocated to the weaker component without documents behind them.
- Residential complaints against ground-floor uses, or the reverse.
- Retail vacancy that drags on lender sizing.
- No clear control of shared systems or parking.
- Seller's pro forma that blends components and hides per-component results.
- Legal structure that prevents separate financing or sale.

## 10. Value levers

- Fix the weakest component or change its use.
- Separate components legally so each can be financed or sold to its best buyer.
- Reallocate shared costs under the documents.
- Sell or recapitalize the component with the highest value to outside buyers.

## 11. Local intelligence

- Read the intelligence section of each component's playbook.

## 12. Model inputs to capture

- Per component: SF, units or keys, rent roll or operating statement, occupancy, revenue, expense, NOI.
- Shared cost schedule, allocation method and percent by component.
- Reserve and capital plan by component, with the shared share.
- Property tax by component, insurance by component.
- Debt by component: amount, test used, rate, term.
- Exit cap rate by component, and sale costs.
- Development or sell-out inputs, if any, from the matching playbook.

## 13. Site visit and management questions

- Walk each component and the shared spaces at different times of day.
- Ask how the owner splits shared costs and who disputes them.
- Ask what the retail tenants say about the residents, and the reverse.
- Ask which shared system needs capital next, and how it will be funded.
